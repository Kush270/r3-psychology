Reading Jodorowsky’s “Psychomagic” critically

Important distinction: Psychomagic is an artistic and philosophical practice associated with filmmaker Alejandro Jodorowsky. It is not an evidence-based psychological treatment, and this article is commentary rather than clinical advice.

Jodorowsky’s writing is provocative because it treats metaphor, ritual and performance as ways of making an internal conflict visible. That can be interesting to read alongside psychotherapy, but an evocative story is not evidence that an intervention is effective or safe.

1. Metaphor can make experience easier to describe

People often use images, objects and stories to communicate feelings that are difficult to put into literal language. Evidence-based therapies may also use imagery or behavioural exercises, but those methods have a clear rationale, agreed boundaries and an opportunity to review what happened.

2. A memorable ritual is not automatically therapeutic

Effort, novelty and emotion can make an experience feel significant. Significance alone does not establish clinical benefit. Any experiential task should be voluntary, proportionate, culturally respectful and considered for possible harm.

3. Family stories can influence identity without determining it

Names, roles and family narratives may shape how people understand themselves. They do not prove a hidden cause of illness, and interpretations should be explored as possibilities rather than presented as facts.

4. Symbolic action should not replace assessment or treatment

There is no sound basis for claiming that a dramatic symbolic act releases trauma, cures illness or resolves violent impulses. Concerns involving trauma, risk, psychosis or severe distress require careful assessment and an appropriate treatment plan.

5. The useful question is collaborative

A safer therapeutic question is not “What extreme act will change me?” but “What does this image or story mean to me, and is there a small, safe action consistent with my values?” The client should understand the purpose, alternatives and limits before agreeing.

When an idea feels powerful, ask three things: What evidence supports it? What are the risks? Does it fit my values and goals?
