Polyvagal theory: a useful lens, with limits

Evidence note: Polyvagal theory is influential in some therapy communities, but several of its anatomical and evolutionary claims remain debated. It is best used as one possible framework, not a settled explanation or diagnosis.

It is common to understand that a room is safe while still feeling tense, watchful or disconnected. Many processes can contribute, including learning history, current stress, trauma, sleep, health, attention and autonomic arousal.

1. Felt safety is more than a thought

Polyvagal-informed language emphasises that safety is experienced through both mind and body. This can help people notice breathing, muscle tension, heart rate, attention and the social environment without treating those sensations as proof of danger.

2. “Neuroception” is a metaphor for detection outside awareness

The theory uses “neuroception” to describe automatic sensitivity to possible threat or safety. It can be a useful conversational shorthand, but it should not be presented as a literal sixth sense or a complete biological account of anxiety.

3. Shutdown and dissociation deserve care, not blame

Freezing, faintness, numbness and dissociation can occur under overwhelming stress. These experiences have multiple possible explanations and warrant individual assessment. A compassionate response does not require claiming that every shutdown response is biologically protective or successful.

4. Connection can help regulation

Supportive voice, expression and presence can influence how safe an interaction feels. Co-regulation may help, but digital contact, solitude and self-regulation are not inherently inferior; what works depends on the person, accessibility and context.

5. Use the model to generate options, not certainty

A practical question might be: “What cues help me feel a little safer right now?” Possible answers include orientation to the room, slower breathing, movement, predictable information, choice, distance or contact with a trusted person. No single exercise is required, and persistent symptoms may need professional assessment.

Which explanation fits your experience, what evidence supports it, and what small response helps without forcing you to ignore discomfort?
